Methodology

Everything you need to defend, attack, or reproduce the ranking.

1. The problem

AI startups sit on a four-decade spread: a small enterprise research lab can sit at $1B valuation, $10M ARR while a foundation-model leader sits at $965B valuation, $100B ARR. Both are arguably "top of the AI market", but they are not directly comparable.

A naive ranking that just sums dollars turns this list into "the companies with the most dollars", which is not interesting: a $4T megacap would always win. A ranking that only uses revenue penalises pre-revenue research labs like Safe Superintelligence and Thinking Machines, which are arguably the most strategically important AI companies in the world.

We want a composite that respects scale of capital (valuation) and scale of revenue (ARR), with a normalisation that does not let the biggest player crush everything else — and that can hold both a pure-play AI lab and a $4 trillion megacap to the same standard.

2. Eligibility: digital AI

A company must be primarily a digital AI business to be ranked. Concretely, we exclude the entire chip, datacenter hardware, humanoid robotics, autonomous vehicles, and physical-world-AI category:

  • Hardware / silicon: NVIDIA, Cerebras, Groq, Tenstorrent, Rebellions, SambaNova, FuriosaAI, Lightmatter, Etched, Positron, MatX, TSMC, Broadcom
  • Datacenter / GPU-cloud infra: CoreWeave, Lambda Labs, Crusoe, Applied Digital, Northern Data, Nebius
  • Robotics: Figure, 1X, Apptronik, Sanctuary, Agility, Skild AI, Physical Intelligence, Skydio
  • Autonomous vehicles: Waymo, Cruise, Zoox, Aurora, Tesla, Mobileye
  • Physical/industrial AI: Anduril, Shield AI, KoBold Metals, Relativity Space, Hadrian
  • No AI revenue line at all: Apple (Apple Intelligence is not separately monetised; a $4.95T market cap would make the valuation axis a measure of consumer-electronics franchises, not AI)

Full exclusion list with rationale →

2b. The admission rule, and the coverage audit

Eligibility is not a matter of taste. A company is admitted if it is:

  • Pure-play digital AI — the AI business is the company, so its market or transaction valuation is already an AI valuation. Its multiple also joins the peer pool.
  • AI-attributed — a diversified company that discloses AI-attributed revenue. That revenue is valued at the peer multiple (section 6b).
  • Market-cap proxy — a company with a material AI business that discloses no AI revenue line, where a market-cap proxy is defensible. Flagged as the weakest evidence in the list.

A software company is excluded when AI is neither disclosed nor material — the Apple test in section 2. Scoring Datadog, AvePoint or Workato on their full market caps would make the valuation axis a measure of observability and backup franchises rather than AI: Datadog's management explicitly declines to size AI revenue at all, and AvePoint's Q2 2026 release discloses SaaS revenue, total revenue and total ARR but no AI line. All three are excluded with reasons on the exclusions page.

The coverage audit — no name may be silently absent

A ranking that only publishes what it included has an invisible hole: a company can be missing simply because nobody checked. The 2026-10 edition was audited against a 39-name third-party AI company list, and the audit is now a repeatable step in the pipeline (scripts/coverage.py, against data/coverage_watchlist.json).

The rule the audit enforces: every watchlist name must resolve to exactly one disposition — ranked, folded into a roll-up row, or excluded with a published reason. A name that resolves to nothing is a coverage gap, and a gap blocks publication.

The audit found 19 names with no disposition at all, plus two roll-ups that had never been written down (Google DeepMind inside Alphabet, Microsoft Research Asia inside Microsoft). All 21 now resolve: 7 were added to the ranking with sourced valuation and AI-attributed ARR, 2 are folded into roll-up rows, and 12 are excluded with a stated reason. The audit now reports 39 of 39 names resolved, zero gaps.

How we treat third-party estimates

A third-party revenue estimate is used as an input only when it is (a) above this list's $10M ARR anchor and (b) the best available figure. It is then published with its source and an explicit confidence flag. Below the anchor it is not used at all — Luma AI's ~$8M Sacra estimate is 22 months old and under the anchor, so that row's ARR axis reads 0 under the missing-ARR rule instead. Where two sources conflict we publish the conservative figure and name the conflict; where a figure is single-sourced we say so.

3. The two input axes

For each eligible company we collect:

  1. Valuation — the most recent primary or tender round, public market cap, or transaction valuation, expressed on a like-for-like AI basis (see section 6b).
  2. AI-attributed ARR — for any company that sells both AI and non-AI products, we take only the AI-attributed revenue. For pure-AI companies, total ARR equals AI-attributed ARR.

Why AI-attributed ARR matters

Summing ARR across a Salesforce ($41.5B total revenue) or ServiceNow ($15.8B total) ranks them above every AI-native competitor, but >90% of that revenue is pre-AI software (CRM, ITSM) with only an incremental "Agentforce" or "Now Assist" slice attributable to the AI wave. That is not a fair comparison against Anthropic ($100B ARR — all of it AI).

Our AI-attribution policy for every company that sells AI alongside non-AI software:

  • Microsoft — $37B AI business ARR (Q3 FY26, +123% YoY). The most transparently disclosed AI revenue figure of any diversified company in this list.
  • Amazon (AWS AI) — $25B+ annual run rate (Q2 2026, triple-digit YoY growth). Excludes the separate silicon/chips business.
  • Google — ~$30B estimate. Alphabet discloses no standalone Gemini revenue, so we estimate the AI-attributable share of the $99B Google Cloud run-rate plus Gemini API and consumer AI subscriptions. Flagged as an estimate.
  • Meta — $20B+ Advantage+ AI advertising campaign run-rate. The Meta AI chatbot and Llama/Muse API generate $0 direct disclosed revenue.
  • Alibaba — $9.3B (AI-related product revenue $1.824B/quarter annualised, plus the AI Labs and Applications segment). Both lines are disclosed.
  • Baidu — $7.4B from its own "Core AI-Powered Business" segment — the most AI-concentrated disclosure of any company in this list (>52% of core revenue).
  • Tencent — ~$7B estimate, from ~40% of Tencent Cloud revenue. Tencent discloses no AI revenue line. Weakest of the big-tech AI figures here.
  • Salesforce — Agentforce ARR $800M (Q4 FY26, +169% YoY). The wider "Agentforce + Data 360" $2.9B figure is partly the acquired Informatica cloud business and doesn't cleanly attribute to AI.
  • ServiceNow — Now Assist + AI portfolio $1.5B FY26 forecast (Q2 2026 actual ACV: $1B). About 10% of total subscription revenue.
  • Notion AI — $300M AI revenue slice (estimate).
  • Snowflake — $100M annualised AI revenue run-rate (company-stated, Dec 2025, not updated at Q2 FY2027). A published AI line, so it is derived rather than proxied — but stale and small against a $114.7B market cap, so we under-credit it rather than guess upward.
  • Palantir, Samsara, Schrodinger, ByteDance, Cohesity, Talkdesk — no AI-attributed revenue disclosed. Handled as market-cap proxies (section 6b).

4. Why log-scale?

Valuation and ARR each span 5+ orders of magnitude across the eligible set. In a linear scale, Anthropic's $100B ARR is over 5,600× SandboxAQ's $17.8M ARR — the smallest AI-attributed revenue figure in the list — and because Anthropic is also the most valuable company in the set, it sits at the top of both axes and owns 100% of each axis's range. The histogram becomes unrecoverable below the giants.

Taking log10 compresses extremes into a much more information-dense scale. After log, Anthropic is at 5.00 and SandboxAQ at 1.25 — a span of 3.75 of the axis's 4 log decades, not 5,600×. This is the standard treatment for financial power-law distributions. 

log_val = log10(valuation_USD_b)
log_arr = log10(arr_USD_m)

5. Min-max rescaling, with fixed bounds

We linearly rescale each axis to [0, 1] using the minimum and maximum log values. The top observation is 1.0, the bottom 0.0. This is the simplest approach and is enough for this kind of heterogeneous cross-sectional ranking. 

z_log(x) = (log10(x) - log_min) / (log_max - log_min)

The bounds are fixed policy, not the sample's own extremes:

  • Valuation: log10 anchored from -1.00 (~$0.1B) to 3.00 (~$1,000B)
  • ARR: log10 anchored from 1.00 (~$10M) to 5.00 (~$100B)

A value below a floor reads 0 — the same as a missing value. The top end is not clamped: a score above 100 is the signal that an anchor has gone stale. Without the floor rule, a company with $5M of ARR would score below a company with no ARR at all, because “no ARR” is scored 0 while a sub-floor figure scores negative. That is a monotonicity break, and on a live list it would surface the first time a very small company joined. The valuation floor earns its keep the same way: raise it from $0.1B to $1B and Otter.ai — a real company with a $0.5B valuation — would drop below companies with no valuation mark at all.

How much room is left on each bound?

All four bounds trace to two companies at the time of the pin: Reworkd AI set the two floors ($0.1B valuation, $10M ARR) and Anthropic set the two ceilings ($1,000B valuation, $100,000M ARR). Reworkd has since fallen out of the Top 100, so the floors now sit above the bottom of the list. That is the fixed-bounds design working as intended — the axis did not move when the composition did — but it leaves the headroom asymmetric: dead space at the bottom, almost none at the top of ARR.

Widening the ARR ceiling is not free — it compresses the ARR axis and shifts the balance back towards valuation.

Why the bounds are fixed

This list is live: it is re-scored as valuations and ARR move. If the bounds were the minimum and maximum observed in the current list, every data update would re-base every other company's score, and the result would be rank churn that has nothing to do with any company having changed.

We measured it, and the measurement is re-runnable rather than a number someone typed once: scripts/measure_bound_churn.py scores the list twice with the real engine — once with sample-derived bounds and once with the shipped anchored bounds — each time with and without a hypothetical entrant. Output: data/bound_churn.json.

Under sample-derived bounds, one company joining at $5M of ARR moves the median score by 2.43 points, re-scores 85 of the other 100 rows, and reorders 17 of them by three or more places — churn that has nothing to do with any company having changed. A $1M-ARR entrant is worse: +6.38 points on the median, a 9.37-point swing on the worst-hit row, and 43 rows reordered. Under the shipped anchored bounds the same two entrants change nothing at all: 0 rows re-scored, 0 rows reordered.

With fixed bounds, none of that happens: the score of a company only moves when that company's own inputs move. And this does not freeze the list — OpenAI overtaking Anthropic still happens correctly. Anchoring removes spurious movement while preserving real movement, which is exactly what a live ranking needs.

Scores are not clipped. A company beyond a bound gets a z-score above 1 and a composite above 100. That is deliberate: it is the signal that the anchor has become stale and needs review, rather than a number that silently pegs.

Re-anchor policy. Bounds are reviewed quarterly and revised only when a company has breached a bound by more than one third of the axis span — an anchor that is about to be crossed rather than one that has been crossed. Every revision is logged with the date, the old bounds, the new bounds and the reason; scores are otherwise comparable across editions. The policy lives in data/model_config.json and is version-controlled with the data.

5b. What if we didn't log? (robustness check)

The log transform is the single most consequential choice in this model, so we tested the alternative. We re-ran the full ranking four ways and compared the results. The code is in scripts/sensitivity.py.

  • A — log + min-max (the shipped model)
  • B — raw + min-max (the naive alternative)
  • C — raw + z-score
  • D — raw + percentile rank (scale-free alternative)

Finding 1: the headline is robust

The top seven are identical under all four variants: Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta, SpaceXAI. Under the scale-free percentile variant (D) the top twelve are identical to the shipped list. If you only care about who's at the top, the scale choice barely matters — and a reasonable reader who dislikes our log transform can check D and find the same answer.

Finding 2: raw min-max silently destroys the bottom 87% of the list

Here's the trap. Raw min-max has a Spearman correlation of +0.919 with the shipped model — it looks fine at a glance. But look at the last column: under raw min-max, 87 of 100 companies score below 5 points out of 100, versus 0 under the shipped model. The list becomes 7 real rows and 93 noise rows. 

Raw min-max, ARR axis (min $10M, max $100,000M):
  Anthropic   $100,000M -> z = 1.0000
  OpenAI       $50,000M -> z = 0.4999
  Microsoft     $37,000M -> z = 0.3699
  a $900M ARR company -> z = 0.0089
  a $12M  ARR company -> z = 0.0002

  A company with $900M ARR and one with $12M ARR are ~75x apart in
  reality, but 0.0089 vs 0.0002 after linear min-max - they are
  indistinguishable on a 0-100 scale.

One company (Anthropic) owns 100% of the ARR axis range, and the second (OpenAI) owns half of what's left. Every ARR below ~$1B is numerically crushed to ~0. That doesn't change who is #1 — it makes the other 93 rows meaningless.

Finding 3: the "improvements" in the raw ranking are artifacts

Raw min-max moves a striking set of companies up: DeepSeek #41→#16, Thinking Machines Lab #42→#17, SSI #50→#22, Reflection AI #56→#26, Moonshot AI #58→#29, Luma AI #93→#58. These are all pre-revenue labs with no ARR at all. Under raw min-max their score collapses to 0.5 × (valuation / $1T) — a number so small it's basically zero. But because the other 87 companies collapsed to even smaller residuals, the zero-revenue labs end up ranked above real revenue businesses.

That's not a better ranking. It's a list where "has no revenue" wins by default. The shipped model has the same effect, but at a scale where the ARR axis still discriminates.

Finding 4: z-score is the worst option

Raw + z-score has the lowest correlation (+0.850) and reorders the top 12. An unbounded standardisation lets a single $965B outlier drag the entire distribution — the mean and standard deviation are set by the giants, and everyone else gets compressed toward zero. It's the worst of the four: 90 of 100 rows score below 5/100. Z-score is the right tool for a symmetric distribution; ARR is a power law.

Finding 5: if you want scale-free, use percentile

Variant D (percentile rank) is the strongest alternative — it correlates more closely with the shipped model (+0.981 Spearman, +0.910 Kendall) than raw min-max does, matches the top 12 exactly, and is invariant to any monotone change of units. It's also the only variant besides the shipped one that keeps every row above 5/100: 0 of 100 rows fall below 5/100 (against 87-90 for the raw variants). Its difference is in the middle: it penalises the proxy rows (Palantir #26→#42, ByteDance #28→#45) because a company with no disclosed ARR genuinely has no ARR, rather than having its market-cap proxy inflate it.

We ship log + min-max rather than percentile because the log model preserves how much bigger one company is than another — a $100B ARR company outranks a $1B ARR company by more than a $1.1B ARR company outranks a $1.0B one, which percentile deliberately flattens. But the two agree closely, and the full comparison is machine-readable at data/sensitivity.json.

6. The composite

Composite score is the equal-weighted average of the two rescaled axes, expressed on a 0-100 scale. The weights are policy, held in data/model_config.json: 

S = 100 * (W_VAL * z_log(val) + W_ARR * z_log(arr))
with W_VAL = W_ARR = 0.5

This is the simplest justifiable weighting that doesn't privilege private-market hype over disclosed revenue (or vice versa). It also makes the score interpretable: a score of 100 means best on both axes; a score of 50 means average on both axes.

Why not a geometric mean?

A geometric mean would punish companies strong on one axis but weak on the other. We deliberately do not do this: pre-revenue labs like SSI and Thinking Machines are strategically important even though their ARR is 0 — a pure valuation signal deserves credit for them. Likewise, high-revenue companies without a recent funding round (e.g. C3.ai, PolyAI, DeepL) are correctly rewarded. The arithmetic mean treats the axes as complements, not substitutes.

Why equal weights? — and what the weighting actually does

The weighting is the most contestable number in the model, so it is measured rather than asserted. It is held in data/model_config.json as policy, and scripts/weight_assessment.py re-runs the whole pipeline at any split. There is a subtlety that changes the whole argument. For a pure-play, valuation and ARR are not independent: valuation is ARR × a multiple. So the composite can be rewritten exactly: 

z_val = z_arr + (log10(multiple) - 1) / 4

S = 100 * ( z_arr  +  w_val * (log10 m - 1) / 4 )
           \__ ARR __/    \__ multiple premium __/

The ARR level carries weight 1.0 at every split. The valuation axis contributes only the multiple premium — how many times ARR the market pays — at w_val / 4 per decade. Across the 75 pure-plays the ARR level term explains 110% of the composite’s variance and the multiple premium 7%. The two axes are negatively correlated, so the premium partly cancels the level term.

The practical consequence: this model is already overwhelmingly an ARR-level model, and “50/50” is not a 50/50 in substance. Re-weighting towards revenue does not shift the balance towards revenue level — the level term is fixed — it shrinks the multiple premium by 20% and, as a side effect, cuts every row with no disclosed ARR by 20% on the axis it does have.

The head of the list does not care: the top 13 is identical at every split, and the top 11 survives the peer multiple at every multiple from 10× to 40×. All the movement is in the mid-table, where scores are densely packed — 28 rows move five or more places on a score change of at most four points.

What the move does is expose a second effect. Under the “missing ARR reads 0” rule, a row with no AI revenue disclosure is scored w_val × z_val only. Raising the ARR weight therefore does not only reward rows that have revenue — it shrinks the axis that the rows without disclosed revenue are scored on. At 0.40 / 0.60, Palantir falls from #26 to #36, ByteDance from #28 to #39, Samsara from #52 to #66, Schrodinger leaves the top 100 entirely, and Safe Superintelligence moves from #50 to #63. That is a disclosure artefact, not a statement about those companies. The weighting and the missing-ARR rule have to be decided together; the rule dominates.

Our call. Keep the equal split. It is the only setting that does not privilege either axis, the lever it moves is small (the valuation axis is 7% of the composite’s variance across pure-plays, not 50%), and the rows it disturbs most are the rows whose ranking already rests on a disclosed weakness rather than on their AI business. If a revenue tilt is wanted, 0.45 / 0.55 is nearly free — ρ 0.995, no row moves ten places — and should be taken at a quarterly policy review, not mid-edition, because it changes every score and breaks comparability with previous editions.

6b. Putting big tech in the list — the three-basis model

This is the hardest problem in the ranking, and the part most likely to be done badly.

Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, Alibaba, Baidu and Tencent have enormous, real, disclosed AI businesses. Excluding them would understate the most important AI businesses on earth. But using their full market caps would let a search-ad business or an e-commerce business win an AI ranking on the strength of non-AI revenue — which is exactly the error we corrected for Salesforce and ServiceNow.

We resolve it by making the valuation axis mean one thing everywhere: "what is this AI business worth?" Three bases:

Basis 1 — Pure-play (market-set AI valuation)

The company is an AI business. Its market or transaction valuation is already an AI valuation, so we use it as-is. Anthropic's $965B is the market's price for Anthropic-the-AI-company. No adjustment.

Basis 2 — AI-attributed (derived)

The company is diversified, but discloses AI-attributed revenue. We take that revenue and value it at the pure-play median EV/Revenue multiple, producing an implied "what would this AI business be worth as a standalone AI company?" figure. 

implied AI valuation = AI-attributed ARR x peer median EV/Revenue

The multiple is computed from the pure-play pool only — so the imputation never feeds back into its own input — and then applied once. For the 2026 set:

  • Pool: 85 pure-play companies with both a valuation and an ARR figure
  • Median multiple: 20.0×
  • Interquartile range: 15× – 40×
  • Range: 5× (Mercor, Otter.ai) to 324× (Zhipu AI)

Worked examples:

Microsoft   $37,000M AI ARR x 20x = $740B implied AI valuation

Compare those to the full-company market caps: Microsoft $3.95T, Alphabet $4.16T, Amazon $2.83T, Meta $1.77T, Tencent $471B, Alibaba $270B, Baidu $27.4B, Salesforce $280B, ServiceNow $200B, Snowflake $114.7B. The gap between each pair is the part of the company that is not AI — and it is usually 95%+ of the market cap. Salesforce's implied AI valuation of $16B against a $280B market cap is a stark case: Agentforce is real, and it is also only ~6% of what the market pays for Salesforce. Snowflake is starker still — $2B of implied AI valuation against a $114.7B market cap is 1.7%.

The cap — a derived valuation can never exceed the company

A derived AI valuation is a claim about a part of a company. A part cannot be worth more than the whole. So the valuation axis carries a hard constraint: 

derived AI valuation = MIN( AI ARR x peer multiple , company market cap )

If the peer multiple implies more than the market values the entire company, the market cap binds and the valuation axis is set there. The uncapped figure is kept and published alongside, so the cap is visible on the site rather than silent, and every capped row is badged in the ranking table.

In the 2026 set exactly one row binds: Baidu. Its $7,440M of AI ARR at 20× implies $148.8B — 543% of Baidu's $27.4B market cap — so Baidu's valuation axis is capped at $27.4B. Notion AI sits exactly at its $6.0B cap (100.0%) without the cap binding. Every other derived row lands between 1.7% and 68.9% of its market cap. The cap costs Baidu exactly one place (#10 → #11) and changes nothing else in the list.

We considered the alternative of only flagging the over-cap rows and publishing an incoherent number, on the grounds that capping imports the market's view of the whole company. We rejected it: a valuation that exceeds the value of the company owning it is not a valuation of anything, and publishing one would quietly let a row rank on a number that cannot exist. Capping keeps the row, keeps the ARR axis intact, and puts the constraint on the axis where it belongs.

Basis 3 — Market-cap proxy

The company discloses no AI-attributed revenue, so no AI valuation can be derived. We use the full market cap as a flagged proxy and score the ARR axis at zero. This simultaneously overstates its AI valuation and understates its AI revenue, so these rows are the weakest evidence in the list and are badged accordingly. Applies to Palantir, Samsara, Schrodinger, ByteDance, Cohesity and Talkdesk.

What this changes

Big tech lands in the top 11 on a like-for-like AI basis — not because search ads are AI, but because once you strip the non-AI business out and value the remainder at peer AI multiples, their AI businesses genuinely are that large. Anthropic and OpenAI remain #1 and #2 because their AI is the whole company, and their AI is worth more per dollar of revenue than anyone else's.

6c. Stress-testing the 20× peer multiple

The 20× multiple is the second-most-consequential assumption in the model (after the log transform), and unlike the log transform it has no natural alternative that agrees with it. So we tested it five ways. Code: scripts/multiple_check.py.

The multiple is pinned, not recomputed

The 20× is the pure-play median as measured when the pin was set, and it is held fixed in data/model_config.json rather than recomputed on every run, for the same reason the axis bounds are fixed: a live list cannot have its derived valuations move because the composition of the peer pool changed. The median is itself a sample-dependent number — adding one company at 90.9× moves the current pool from 20.83× to 21.08× and shifts every derived megacap row.

The current pool (n=85, IQR 15–40×, mean 37.7×, trimmed mean 27.3×) computes to a median of 20.83× — a move of 0.83× from the pinned 20.0×, and far inside the one-third-of-an-IQR retune trigger of about 8×. The pin therefore stands, and no derived row moved as a result of this update.

Review policy. The pinned value is re-reviewed quarterly against the current pool, and revised only when the pool median has moved more than one third of the IQR (about 8×) from the pinned value — not merely because one company joined. The published distribution above always shows the current pool next to the pinned value, so the pin can never drift away from the data unnoticed. The stress test below is re-run on every review.

The two pure-plays actually comparable in scale to Microsoft's $37B AI business — Anthropic ($100B ARR) and OpenAI ($50B ARR) — trade at 9.7× and 17.0×. A size-matched multiple would be closer to 13×, not 20×.

This is a real limitation and we won't hide it: applying 20× to a $37B ARR business is generous relative to the scale-matched cohort. A size-matched 13× would put Microsoft at ~$481B rather than $740B, and Google at ~$390B rather than $600B. We keep 20× because the blended median is the most defensible single summary of a heterogeneous pool, and because — as Test 5 shows — the choice does not change who ranks where.

Three findings here.

First: the market pays 67–139× for AI revenue at the megacaps. That is far above the 20× pure-play median — because the market is pricing AI optionality (the option to reshape search, advertising, cloud and office), not just the revenue itself. Our derived valuation deliberately strips that optionality out, which is exactly what makes the ranking a statement about AI businesses rather than about megacap option value. Salesforce at 350× is the most extreme megacap case: the market is paying enormously for Agentforce optionality on a $280B cap, while we value the $800M of actual Agentforce ARR at $16B.

Second: Snowflake is the extreme case at 1,147×. Its $100M of disclosed AI revenue is 1.7% of a $114.7B market cap, so the market is paying for the data platform and the AI story, not for the AI revenue. That is the clearest illustration of why a disclosed AI line and a full market cap are not interchangeable — and why we publish the AI figure on its own.

Third: Baidu is the one row where the derivation overruns the company. Uncapped, our derivation implies $149B for Baidu's AI business — 5.4× Baidu's entire market cap. A component cannot be worth more than the whole. The most likely explanation is that Baidu's "Core AI-Powered Business" line (RMB 12.5B/quarter, >52% of core revenue) is substantially re-segmented legacy search and advertising rather than a new AI business — the same relabelling problem we corrected for Salesforce. The market's 3.7× implied multiple is telling us it refuses to pay a tech multiple for that. The valuation cap therefore binds: Baidu's valuation axis is set to its $27.4B market cap, its uncapped $148.8B is published alongside it, and the row is badged as indicative only.

Test 5 — does the ranking survive the multiple?

We re-ran the entire ranking at multiples of 10×, 12×, 15×, 20×, 25×, 30× and 40×.

The top 11 is identical at every multiple from 10× to 40×. The same eleven names, in nearly the same order: Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta, SpaceXAI, Alibaba, Databricks, Tencent, Baidu. Only the internal ordering inside that block shifts — at 40× OpenAI drops below Microsoft, at 10× Alibaba drops out of the top 8 in favour of Databricks.

The cap applies at every multiple too, so Baidu sits at its $27.4B market cap in all seven runs and cannot be inflated by a higher multiple. That makes the top-11 result stronger, not weaker: the ranking survives the multiple and the coherence constraint at the same time.

So the conclusion "these are the top AI companies on a valuation-and-revenue basis" is robust to the multiple, which is the strongest thing we can say about a single-number assumption in a composite score. The number affects the derived dollar values, not the ordering.

7. Missing data rules

Two kinds of missing data are common in 2026:

  1. Valuation missing, ARR present (e.g. Midjourney, Suno, Aleph Alpha). These are mature, profitable businesses that have no need for outside capital. The valuation axis is set to 0 (worst observed), so they compete on the ARR axis only.
  2. ARR missing, valuation present (e.g. DeepSeek, SSI, Thinking Machines, Magic AI). These are pre-revenue frontier labs whose capital signal is the most important available. The ARR axis is set to 0, and they compete on valuation only.
  3. Both missing. We drop them entirely.
  4. Below the axis floor (e.g. $5M of ARR against a $10M floor). These read 0 on that axis, exactly like a missing value — “no usable signal” rather than “worse than no signal”. See section 5.

This is a permissive rule. A strict alternative would be to lower missing values to a sentinel below the observed minimum. We chose 0 because it is interpretable and makes the missing-data penalty visible in the score.

8. Worked example: Anthropic

Anthropic is the #1 company in the 2026 ranking. Inputs:

valuation_USD_b = 965
arr_USD_m       = 100000   (> $100B annualised run-rate, mid-Sep 2026)

After log10:

log_val = 2.985
log_arr = 5.000

Rescaled against the fixed bounds (valuation $0.1B–$1,000B, ARR $10M–$100B):

z_val = (2.985 − (−1.00)) / (3.00 − (−1.00)) = 0.9961
z_arr = (5.000 − 1.00) / (5.000 − 1.00) = 1.0000

Composite:

S = 100 * (0.5 * 0.9961 + 0.5 * 1.0000) = 99.81 / 100

Anthropic sits at the top because it is at the very top of both axes: the highest valuation in the set and the highest AI-attributed ARR in the set. Its score is 99.81 rather than 100 because the valuation anchor is $1,000B and Anthropic is at $965B — which is the intended behaviour. A score of 100 means "at the top of the anchored scale"; a score above 100 means "past the anchor, and the anchor is stale". OpenAI is #2 (z_val 0.983, z_arr 0.925 → 95.37).

And a derived example: Microsoft

AI ARR                  = $37,000M  (disclosed, Q3 FY26)
peer multiple (pinned)    = 20.0x     (pure-play median, 2026-10 edition, n=85)
implied AI valuation     = $37,000M x 20.0x = $740B
log_val = log10(740) = 2.869
z_val   = (2.869 − (−1.00)) / 4.00 = 0.967
log_arr = log10(37000) = 4.568
z_arr   = (4.568 − 1.00) / 4.00 = 0.892
S       = 100 * (0.5 * 0.967 + 0.5 * 0.892) = 92.97  → rank #3

10. Limitations

  • Stale numbers. Private-market round prices change fast. We use the latest figure we could verify through October 2026.
  • Annualised vs. real. Many companies publish "run-rate ARR" rather than booked ARR. We accept run-rate but flag it in the source column.
  • Two of the big-tech AI figures are estimates, not disclosures. Google (~$30B) and Tencent (~$7B) do not publish AI revenue. Their rows are flagged estimated in the source column. Every other big-tech AI figure is a company disclosure.
  • The peer multiple is a single number. 20× is a median across 85 companies spanning $10M to $100B ARR. A 15× multiple would put Microsoft at #3 with a $555B implied valuation instead of $740B, and would not change the identity of the top 3. A 40× multiple (the p75) would lift the big-tech rows further.
  • The log transform is load-bearing. Section 5b tests the alternative. The top 7 are identical under every variant tested, but raw min-max would push 87 of 100 rows below 5/100 and make "no disclosed revenue" rank above real revenue businesses by default. The choice is documented rather than assumed.
  • Derived valuations are not market prices. A "derived" valuation of $740B for Microsoft's AI business is an analytical construct, not something you can sell. It answers "what would this be worth at peer AI multiples", not "what is it worth today".
  • Proxy rows are weak. Palantir, Samsara, Schrodinger, ByteDance, Cohesity and Talkdesk disclose no AI-attributed revenue and are scored on full market cap with a zero ARR axis. Treat their ranks as indicative only.
  • Category boundaries. Some companies straddle categories (Databricks = data + AI; Scale AI = data labeling + defense). They sit where they sit on company self-description.
  • Recent M&A. Several big movers in 2025-2026 are now subsidiaries of larger companies, and their valuations are the last reported private round before the deal closed.
    • SpaceXAI (xAI + Cursor + X) — xAI acquired by SpaceX all-stock for $250B, closed Feb 2, 2026; Cursor acquired all-stock for $60B, signed Jun 16, 2026 and closed Aug 14, 2026. Presented as one consolidated entity with a combined $310B valuation (the sum of the two disclosed transaction prices) and ~$14.2B ARR.
    • Codeium / Windsurf — acquired by Cognition, undisclosed (~$250M est.), closed Jul 14, 2025 with $82M ARR. Dropped as a standalone row because its ARR is now inside Cognition's consolidated figure.
    • Hugging Face — acquired by NVIDIA in 2025; its $12.93B is the acquisition price, which remains a valid AI valuation.
